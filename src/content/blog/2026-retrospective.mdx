A year ago I closed [my 2025 retrospective](/blog/2025-retrospective-2026-outlook) with three promises, written in the language of someone who had just discovered impact matrices. By the end of 2026 I would know whether I wanted to become a manager or a Staff Engineer, I would share what I learn consistently on the blog, on LinkedIn and in a newsletter, and I would stick with Ju Jutsu. Next to them sat two anti-goals: no speaking on stage just for the sake of it, only when it's fun, and no standing still.

I reread it this week, and the scorecard is a strange one. I kept the Ju Jutsu promise in full, I kept the one about sharing only in the second half of the year, and the big career question I didn't answer at all.

The rest of this post is about why I couldn't answer it, because the reason turned out to be the same thing that made this year good.

## The question I couldn't answer

At the end of 2025 I wrote that by now I wanted a clearer answer to "Manager or Staff Engineer?". I don't have one, and I've stopped thinking the question still makes sense the way I asked it.

This year AI took most of the code off my desk. I'm still a programmer, but I rarely sit down and write a feature line by line anymore. The work looks more like being a very technical product person: I decide what has to be built, I instruct the agents, I work out how they should test what they produce and how the pieces should fit together, and that last part needs more architecture knowledge than I ever needed when I was typing everything myself. There's a sad side to it that I don't want to skip. Part of the energy I got from this job came from the feeling of making something with my own hands, and that feeling is much thinner now.

If the hard coding problems end up being solved by the machines, I struggle to see what keeps a Staff or Principal Engineer, the people whose job is to make other engineers better, from doing most of what a manager does. The hours they used to spend in the code are gone, and the most useful thing left for them is the team. My guess is that the two paths are going to merge into one role. I might be completely wrong, and I'm not claiming to know where this ends up, but it's the most honest answer I have to my own question.

In practice I'm already doing a mix of both: the people side of a manager and the enabling side of a staff engineer. The second one is where I'm weakest, because I still carry what I call the "I'll close it myself" syndrome. I'm often the fastest person to finish a task, so I finish it, even when it was something someone else could have done and learned from. I've caught myself doing it more and more this year, and I still haven't made the switch from "I close things because I'm fast" to "I make the others as fast as me". Part of the reason is a loop that's hard to break: when you're not the one closing tickets, it looks like you're not doing anything, and on the busy days I see it that way too, because I also want to pick something up and ship it. It's probably no accident that the book I'm reading right now is *The Manager's Path*.

The way I review code changed for the same reason. With agents in the loop there are too many lines, too many PRs, arriving too fast, and a reviewer who reads every line becomes the bottleneck for the whole team. I've started reviewing intentions more than code: the code can be rewritten in minutes, while a feature built on the wrong idea has to be stopped before it goes anywhere. (I think this one deserves a post of its own.)

## Ideas got cheap

The sadness about not building with my hands has a flip side, and I see it in my side projects more than at work. The satisfaction is still there, it just moved. What I enjoy now is watching an idea of mine work, and I can get there in an afternoon. Before, I had plenty of ideas, and most of them died because I didn't have the time or the energy to sit down and build them.

So this year I built more than ever, and I wrote about the odd consequences of that in [I Built a Tool I Don't Use](/blog/i-built-a-tool-i-dont-use). It's the same shift I described for reviews, seen from the other side: the code stopped being the expensive part, and the idea behind it became the thing worth spending time on. If you've read [Stop Prompting. Start Thinking.](/blog/stop-prompting-start-thinking) you've already seen where that leads.

This is also where the promise about sharing lives, and the first half of the year was quiet: one post in March, one in April, then two in July. The consistency arrived in the second half: the newsletter started in July, and from October there has been a post every month, most of them parts of a blog series. I'll take the win, late as it is.

## Back on stage

The anti-goal about the stage is the one I can call a clean success. I sent three CFPs and, to my surprise, all three were accepted, and all three talks landed in the last three months of the year: ComeToCode in Pignola in September, GoLab in Bologna at the start of November, and reactjsday in Verona a couple of weeks later. I had missed it.

![Me at GoLab 2026 in Bologna, mid-talk with the clicker in my hand, wearing the ComeToCode 2026 t-shirt](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/golab.webp)

ComeToCode is the one I keep thinking about. Pignola is a small village on a hill in the province of Potenza, and getting there is a small pilgrimage: I took a flight and then a lift by car, and many of the people I met had driven for hours from their own cities. It's nothing like arriving in Milan or Bologna by train and walking to the venue. And yet it was full, it felt like a real community, and some of the people I met there I found again at the next conferences, which made those conferences better too. On the way home I finally saw Naples, even if only for a day.

![The ComeToCode 2026 group photo: about a hundred people in a cobbled square in Pignola, standing, crouching and lying on the stones in front of an old pink building, under a blue sky](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/pignola.webp)

Then there's the other side of the stage, the one I see as an organiser. With [Schrodinger Hat](https://schroedinger-hat.org) we ran Open Source Day 2026 in Florence in April, and I'm proud of it: it was our biggest edition ever for people in the room, and from the feedback we heard, maybe the most appreciated one. Getting there was hard, though. For weeks the tickets wouldn't move, for a free event that only asks you to take a train and a day or two off, and then at some point it unlocked. The workshops drew very little interest, and that one stings a bit, since I was giving one of them. My strong take, which I hold loosely, is that with AI around, sitting in a room to follow a workshop step by step is losing its point.

I hear the same thing from speakers and organisers I meet around: fewer people go to conferences. I honestly don't know why. Maybe people are tired, maybe they're bored of seeing the same talks, maybe the technical content is something AI now gives them on demand. What I do see is that the events built around a community, ComeToCode, the unconferences, our own Open Source Day in the end, keep working, while the traditional ones struggle more. My guess is that people are looking for the human part, and I'm one of them: the beer after the meetup, the chat at the conference bar that goes on for an hour, people who want to build things together and learn silly things from each other.

## Verona is going quiet

That's why one trend makes me sad. I live in Verona, and Verona is going quiet. The few conferences left here are reactjsday, Angular Day, PHP Day and Laravel Day, and reactjsday is moving to Bologna, with Angular Day probably following. Most of the events are leaving for Bologna, and living here I'm slowly losing the meetups, the events and, with them, the human side I just described.

So there's one thing I'd really like to do in 2027, and it's to bring something back to Verona.

## A year of first times

Outside of work, 2026 was a year of first times, and almost all of them taught me the same lesson as ComeToCode: being there is a different thing from watching it on a screen.

In January I signed up for Ju Jutsu, and this year I earned my first belt. It's the one 2025 promise I kept in full.

![The Ju Jutsu group in white gis on the mats of the dojo, the new yellow belts kneeling in the front row](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/ju-jutsu.webp)

I grew up on WWE. I watched Raw and SmackDown every week and played *SmackDown vs. Raw 2006* when wrestling was everywhere in Italy, and I had never seen a show live. This year WWE held its first ever pay-per-view in Italy, with a run of dates around the country, and I missed the pay-per-view because I hadn't thought about it and the tickets felt like a lot of money. A few days before the Bologna date I realised that I had wanted to see this since I was a kid, and I went to SmackDown with my girlfriend. Live, they're incredible athletes, and seeing Cody Rhodes, Rhea Ripley and Charlotte Flair in the same arena was a joy. I'd go back, even though I barely follow it anymore.

![The arena in Bologna before SmackDown, the ring under the lights and the WWE logos glowing over the crowd](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/smackdown.webp)

Music had its first time too: I finally saw Caparezza live, in Mantua.

The last one happened a few days before I started writing this. My brother and I gave our dad tickets to the ATP Finals in Turin, and I went with him. It was the first time I watched tennis live, and we didn't get just anyone: we got Carlos Alcaraz. On TV you have no idea how hard they hit the ball, and the doubles match was even more fun to watch than I expected. The best part, though, was my dad, who had the time of his life.

![The ATP Finals court in Turin seen from the top of the stands, a blue court in a full, dark arena](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/atp-finals.webp)

Travel was simple this year: a few days in the mountains in Alto Adige, with a stop at Lake Braies, and a trip down to Calabria to see my girlfriend's parents.

![Lake Braies in Alto Adige on a clear autumn morning, the rock face reflected in the still water](/images/blog/2026-retrospective/alto-adige.webp)

The rest of my year off the clock (I've started reading American comics, and I'm hooked) is on [/now](/now).

## 2027

I don't know what 2027 holds, and I don't know yet whether there will be an Open Source Day 2027. I know what I want, though. I want to bring events back to Verona, keep running the community, keep travelling to speak, and do more things live, like the ones above. And there's one thing I want to stop doing: closing everything myself because I'm the fastest. It's the only 2025 promise still half done, so it gets another year.

I'm also still curious about the question I couldn't answer about conferences. If you know why people go to fewer of them, or you stopped going yourself, I'd really like to hear it on [Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/davideimola.dev) or [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/davideimola/).
